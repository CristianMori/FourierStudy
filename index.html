Sampling a square wave: Nyquist, aliasing and the anti-alias filter

A square wave is a sum of sine waves at f0, 3·f0, 5·f0, … that never ends. Sample it, and every harmonic above fs/2 folds back into the band 0…fs/2. Filter those harmonics out first, and the samples give the signal back exactly.

What this shows

The sampling theorem says: if a signal has no frequency content at or above fs/2, its samples determine it completely, and ideal reconstruction gives it back exactly. That holds for every signal, square waves included. The theorem has a condition, though, and a square wave does not meet it.

A square wave of frequency f0 is the sum of sine waves at f0, 3·f0, 5·f0, … with amplitudes 4/(πn). The series never ends, so the square wave is not band-limited: whatever fs you choose, it has harmonics above fs/2. Those harmonics do not vanish when you sample. They show up in the samples as other frequencies inside 0…fs/2 (the red stems). That is aliasing, and it is what the theorem predicts when its condition is broken.

Now switch on the anti-alias filter. It removes every harmonic at or above the cutoff before sampling, so the condition holds. The reconstruction then matches the filtered signal to rounding error, Gibbs ringing included. The ringing comes from cutting off the harmonics, not from sampling.

So the honest summary is: a perfect square wave cannot be sampled without aliasing, because it is not band-limited. That is not an exception to Nyquist; it is Nyquist.

How it is computed: the square wave is sign(sin 2πf0t) = Σ (4/πn)·cos(2πnf0t − 90°) over odd n. A harmonic at f = n·f0 with phase φ, sampled at tk = (k + offset)/fs, gives the same samples as a component at f − m·fs with phase φ + 2π·m·offset, where m = round(f/fs). If that frequency is negative it is mirrored to positive and its phase changes sign. Components landing on the same frequency are added as complex phasors; at exactly fs/2 only the part the samples can see survives. The reconstruction is the sum of these baseband components, which is the same as ideal sinc interpolation of the samples. The sum uses odd harmonics up to 40·fs (at least 200, at most 8000 of them).
